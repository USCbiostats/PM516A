# Presenting Statistical Information: Group Activity

In small groups, share the misleading figure you found and the table or figure you prepared for your consulting project. Use these examples to find matches for the categories below. To claim **BINGO**, complete a row, column, or diagonal, and be ready to explain why each example qualifies. The center square is a pseudo-free space that you can earn by identifying what makes your project table effective.

## Bingo Card

| | | |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **The Lie Factor** | **A Picture Is Worth 1,000 Words** | **We Should Break Up** |
| **Chartjunk** | **Table 1-derful** <br>⭐ | **Wrong Tool for the Job** |
| **Where's the Uncertainty?** | **Know Your Audience** | **Ambiguous, Much?** |


## Category Glossary

1. **The Lie Factor:** A graph that exaggerates or minimizes the apparent magnitude of a difference, trend, or effect through its visual design (such as a distorted scale or disproportionate symbols). Be prepared to compute or estimate the [value of the lie factor](https://infovis-wiki.net/wiki/Lie_Factor). 

2. **A Picture Is Worth 1,000 Words:** A well-designed figure that communicates an important and potentially complex finding more clearly or efficiently than a lengthy written explanation or table would. Be prepared to share how many words you think this figure saves in text.

3. **We Should Break Up:** A graph that lacks clear axis labels, units, or a necessary legend, making its meaning difficult or impossible to interpret. (Inspired by [xkcd #833](https://xkcd.com/833/).) Be prepared to share different interpretations of the axis that would be fair for the reader to make.

4. **Chartjunk:** A figure with unnecessary decoration, 3D effects, distracting colors, or visual clutter that compete with the data rather than helping readers understand them. Be prepared to share steps you would take to fix the figure.

5. **Table 1-derful:** A descriptive table that presents the essential information clearly, with well-organized rows and columns, meaningful labels, appropriate summary statistics, and understandable denominators or units. Be prepared to justify why your table is *wonderful*.

6. **Wrong Tool for the Job:** A presentation format that obscures the message: a graph would work better as a table or text, a table would work better as a figure, or a different type of graph would be more appropriate. Be prepared to justify why the chosen format is suboptimal.

7. **Where's the Uncertainty?:** A figure that presents statistical estimates without important information about their precision (when relevant), or uses error bars without explaining whether they represent standard deviations, standard errors, confidence intervals, or something else. Be prepared to explain what information is missing and why it would be important to include.

8. **Know Your Audience:** A technically correct table or figure that is poorly matched to its intended readers—for example, using specialized statistical notation without explanation for a nonstatistical audience. Be prepared to explain what changes should be made for interpretability, and for which intended audience.

9. **Ambiguous, Much?:** A table or figure omits information needed to understand what was analyzed or how—for example, reporting p-values without identifying the tests used, adjusted estimates without listing adjustment variables, or results without defining the comparison groups. Be prepared to explain what information is missing and why it would be important to include.
